  • Physical Principles for Scaling Neural Decoding

    • Let’s say you want to build magical brain interfaces.

    The problem boils down to three components:

    The number of bits needed to record every single neuron in the human brain per second seems like an intractable problem. But it an be estimated as follows with a few underlying assumptions.

    Total data rate = number of neurons in a human brain * frequency of sampling * bits per sample

    = 86 billion neurons * 1000Hz * 1 bit per sample = 86 trillion bits per second = 11 Tb / second

    that's a lot of bits!!

    Bottleneck #1: information compression and transmission

    Human brain tissue can withstand 2°C change in temperature over hours, before serious tissue damage.

    Instantaneous change in temperature of brain tissue wrt time = heat transfer of deep brain tissue / brain tissues heat capacity.

    Heat transfer of brain tissue: = (applied power + power per unit mass of basal metabolism) - density*heat capacity*volume of rate of flow*change in temperature of blood.

    Solve for applied power to get the heat generated must be less than 2.5 times the energy generated by the human brain.

    that's a lot of heat!!

    Bottleneck #2: heat generated from electrodes, neural recording, processing, and transmission

    The volume of electrodes and hardware implanted into the brain must displace <1% of the total brain.
    The wire can’t be too thin, otherwise it isn’t physically feasible to construct it without it giving unreliable signals. But the number of electrodes can’t be too little, otherwise we can’t distinguish the neurons from one another.

    that’s a lot of wires in your brain!!

    Bottleneck #3: the hardware implants connected to the brain has to displace <1% of total brain volume, before sustaining permanent, irreversible brain damage.

    Great! Now you have a somewhat broad understanding of what brain computer interface researchers are consistently asking themselves every time they build new select modalities to understand our thoughts. How scalable is it? How invasive is it? What's the bandwidth and power assumptions? What's the spatial and temporal resolution of this technology? To get a sense of how whole brain emulation works for humans, bci researchers reason from smaller, controllable environments, including mouse brains, then scale up using assumptions. A few questions:

    1. How many bits per second are needed to record every single neuron in a mouse brain?
    2. How much total power in Watts or milliWatts can be dissipated within the volume of a mouse brain, about 0.5 grams, before the temperature rises more than 2°C?
    3. How many electrodes do we need to clearly distinguish one neuron from another? How small do the diameter of the wires need to be?

  • Quantum Computing for the very curious

    • In classical computing, bits encode information as 0s and 1s. In quantum computing, qubits store quantum states, defined as \(2\)-dimensional unit vectors living in complex space. The vector is composed from a linear combination of elementary states, the zero ket (\(\vert{}0\rangle\)) and the one ket (\(\vert{}1\rangle\)). Quantum systems are composed of qubits, wires, and quantum gates. Just as a classical computer can be configurable by different physical systems, quantum systems operate and behave differently using different components, but the underlying principles are the same. A transition through a quantum wire represents a passage in time. Qubits can be represented by any particle, such as a proton, photon, or neutrino. However, some particles are more suited to be qubits than wires and vice versa. Neutrinos have weak interactions with other matter, making it hard for them to manipulate stable systems. Thus, neutrinos are suitable to be used as quantum wires but are poor candidates to be used as qubits.

    Max Planck introduced energy quantization in 1900. Einstein expanded the quantum idea to light, explaining the photoelectric effect in 1905. Niels Bohr modeled the atomic structure, where electrons occupied fixed orbits in 1913. Then in 1925, Werner Heisenberg formalized the field with matrix mechanics. Decades later, Alan Turing was trying to prove the Entscheidungsproblem, or the decision problem, proposed by David Hilbert. Building on computing theory, David Deutsch predicted that classical computers cannot simulate quantum processes. He devised the first quantum algorithm that is exponentially faster than any classical algorithm, using a single query instead of two queries using classical means.

    When calculating these quantum processes, unitary matrices are essential because they are the only matrices that preserve the length of a vector. Taking the complex transpose of an arbitrary quantum state ket produces a bra, which we denote as \(\langle\psi\vert{}\). The matrix product of a unitary matrix and its Hermitian conjugate, given by the dagger notation, rotates the vector in space but preserves the length of the vector. This is known as Dirac notation, or bra-ket notation. To visualize this concretely, think of how a matrix transforms a vector in space: the operation \(M\vert{}e_k\rangle\) physically maps to the \(k\)-th column of matrix \(M\). Extending this visual logic to pinpointing exact matrix entries, \(M_{jk} = \langle e_j\vert{} M \vert{}e_k\rangle\). In other words, finding the entry of a matrix is the exact equivalent of applying the elementary row vector \(\langle e_j\vert{}\) to the matrix \(M\), followed by applying the column vector \(\vert{}e_k\rangle\). The norm of the ket is the square root of the product of the bra-ket.

    Using this mathematical definition, we can venture into quantum gates involving two qubits. The geometric intuition for applying quantum gates on qubits is the rotation of the state vector on the 3D surface of a sphere called the Bloch Sphere. The computational basis states for a 2-qubit system are \(\vert{}00\rangle\), \(\vert{}01\rangle\), \(\vert{}10\rangle\), and \(\vert{}11\rangle\). The CNOT gate is the quantum equivalent of the NOT gate for two qubits. This flipping mechanism is used as follows: define the control qubit and target qubit. Suppose \(\vert{}x\rangle\) is the control and \(\vert{}y\rangle\) is the target. The gate transforms the state such that \(\vert{}xy\rangle \rightarrow \vert{}x, y \oplus x\rangle\). This means we are modulo the addition of \(x\) and \(y\). If \(x\) is 0, the switch is off, thus the state of \(y\) doesn’t change. If \(x\) is 1, then \(y\) flips. Next is the Toffoli gate, the equivalent of the AND gate in classical computing. Because qubits have to be unitary and reversible, \((x \land y)\) is very difficult to implement in quantum computing. The NAND gate can be constructed by taking apart the \(z\) component of the Toffoli gate and replacing \(z\) with 1. Ultimately, quantum computing consists of three parts: the input being the initial computational basis state, passing through a quantum circuit composed of CNOT gates and other quantum gates, whose output is measured by a classical bit.

    The holy grail of quantum computing is to simulate any physical system, though simulating the universe remains incredibly hard because it is complex, messy, and nondeterministic. So far, physicists are unable to bridge the gap between the standard model and Einstein’s general relativity, though John Preskill and his collaborators have made progress using quantum computing to simulate quantum field theory. In this realm, quantum systems could potentially simulate subatomic particle interactions and collisions, guide smart materials to change form on command, or coordinate tiny systems to build or fix objects.

    Furthermore, atom behavior at the quantum level is very poorly simulated by chemists, making drug discovery slow. In drug discovery, quantum algorithms could model chemical bonds and atoms, speed up tests for drug compounds, and cut down trial and error costs in labs.

    On the geopolitical front, intelligence agencies have been interested in the development of quantum computing to crack encryptions since the mid-1990s in hopes of gathering intelligence. An proven result of quantum computing is Shor’s quantum factorization algorithm devised by American theoretical computer scientist Peter Shor. Shor's algorithm can factorize any positive real number, in the hundreds of digits, to its prime factors in polynomial time versus the best classical algorithms that can only do so in sub-exponential time. Theoreticially it can unlock widely used security keys and force global organizations to adopt quantum-safe encryption, yet to be practically useful, breaking real-world secure encryption keys, such as 2048-bit RSA key, requires millions of qubits. Quantum decoherence and high error rates have contained progress on the number of qubits from 2 in 1997 (IBM, Oxford, UC Berkeley, Stanford, MIT) to 120 (IBM Nighthawk 2025) over the past 20 years. While quantum physicists can agree on the underlying math, physical interpretations vastly differ, and the societal implications are much more uncertain. Even though large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers remain decades away, our security risks and economic inequalities remain heavily dependent on the proactive migration to post-quantum cryptography today.
